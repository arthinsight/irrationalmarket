What the company does

Engineered-to-order thermal products for heavy industry — sold into metal, petrochemical, power, defence and glass plants.

Tempsens designs and manufactures customised temperature sensors, electrical heating elements and specialised cables. Per the Frost & Sullivan report the company commissioned, it is the largest Indian manufacturer of contact and non-contact temperature sensors by revenue, with roughly 10.5% of the temperature-sensor segment and about 21.3% of non-contact sensors in FY26 — where it says it is the only Indian manufacturer.

The business traces to a partnership firm set up in Udaipur in 1984. It now runs 15 manufacturing units with its joint ventures — ten in Udaipur, five overseas in the UAE, Korea, Indonesia, Germany and Poland — employs 814 people, and has shipped to more than 80 countries over the last three years. Roughly two-thirds of revenue comes from project and OEM orders; the rest is replacement supply.

Balance-sheet strength 6

₹78.0 cr of debt against ₹496.9 cr of net worth, debt/EBITDA 0.69×, contingent liabilities 0.35% of net worth, rated [ICRA]A. Held back because working-capital lines are 82.6% of borrowings and the FX book is explicitly unhedged.

Quality of growth 4

Revenue rose from ₹274.8 cr to ₹444.9 cr in two years, but FY25 absorbed Marathon Heater and FY26 absorbed the German, Polish and Measurement & Control businesses. The issuer states the three years are not comparable, so the organic rate is not readable.

Return profile 4

RoE 20.0% → 14.1% → 13.5% as the acquired asset base landed. The flattering 20.7% “adjusted” RoE is struck on equity that excludes the goodwill and intangibles those acquisitions created.

Disclosure completeness 4

Operational disclosure is unusually granular — capacity by unit, supplier bands, litigation, related parties. But no order book is quantified anywhere, and no top-1 or top-5 customer share is given.

Cash conversion 3

Operating cash flow has fallen behind profit for three straight years — ₹42.0 cr against ₹71.1 cr in FY26. Working-capital days went 152 → 193 → 210; inventory days 125 → 170.

Objects and execution readiness 3

₹18.1 cr of capex with no definitive agreements — firm purchase orders cover only part of it, the rest rests on quotations, several expiring around listing. No monitoring agency is required and no object has been appraised.

Governance and disclosure 3

A ₹11.0 cr claim against a subsidiary bought four months before filing; 50% of the German subsidiary bought from the managing director; group companies free to compete with no non-compete; untraceable statutory filings from 2004 and 1990.

Selling-shareholder alignment 2

Every one of the 1.85 crore shares on offer comes from holders whose acquisition cost is nil to ₹0.06 a share. The two promoters who paid real money — ₹55.63 and ₹37.43 — sell nothing.

What stands up

  • Low leverage. ₹78.0 cr of borrowings against ₹496.9 cr of net worth. ICRA upgraded the rating to [ICRA]A in August 2024 and reaffirmed it in November 2025.
  • Exports compounding. Revenue from outside India went ₹58.6 cr → ₹125.8 cr across FY24–FY26, a 46.5% CAGR, now 28.5% of the top line.
  • Little customer concentration. The top ten customers are 18.6% of FY26 revenue, down from 24.7% in FY24 — unusual for an industrial business this size.
  • Real qualification barriers. ASME “U” stamp, NABL ISO/IEC 17025 calibration lab, ATEX, IECEx, UL, CE and PESO approvals, 12 granted Indian patents, and in-house alloy melting, hot rolling and machining.
  • Cross-selling is measured, not asserted. ₹266.8 cr — 60.0% of FY26 revenue — came from customers buying across more than one product line. The top twenty customers average ten years of association.
  • Clean operating record. No shutdowns, fires, material warranty claims, guarantee invocations, order cancellations, covenant breaches or lost accreditations across FY24–FY26.

What to watch

  • Profit is not becoming cash. Operating cash flow covered 91% of profit in FY24, 87% in FY25 and 59% in FY26. Inventory reached ₹111.9 cr on a ₹444.9 cr revenue base.
  • Most of the fresh money is not new capacity. Of ₹95 cr raised, ₹55 cr repays working-capital lines and ₹18.1 cr funds capex. That capex adds 7.6% to heating capacity and 11.8% to cable capacity.
  • Growth was bought. ₹190.2 cr of FY25's ₹250.0 cr increase in net worth came from the Marathon Heater amalgamation, which also created ₹106.1 cr of goodwill.
  • New capacity is barely running. FY26 utilisation was 18.5% in Poland, 20.6% in Germany, 21.9% in Korea and 41–42% at Units V and VI. Unit I fell from 80.6% to 58.1%.
  • A related-party acquisition inside the accounts. Half of the German subsidiary was bought from promoter and managing director Vinay Rathi for €763,500 — and it had been a top-ten customer before the purchase.
  • One large unquantified legal exposure. A ₹11.0 cr interim-relief claim before the Bombay High Court against a subsidiary acquired on 30 March 2026, against which all other litigation totals ₹1.8 cr.

Questions the document leaves open

Each of these is answerable from the company's first post-listing disclosures.

  • How much of the ₹111.9 cr inventory build is stock consolidated from the four acquired businesses, and how much is organic stocking against undelivered orders?
  • On what independent valuation was half of the German subsidiary acquired from the managing director, and why does control rest on a voting agreement rather than a majority stake?
  • Excluding the FY26 consolidation of Germany and Poland, what was organic revenue growth and organic export growth?
  • With working-capital lines at 82.6% of borrowings and a 210-day cycle, what stops the ₹55 cr being repaid from the offer from simply being redrawn within a year?
  • No order book is quantified anywhere in the RHP, on a business where two-thirds of revenue is project and OEM work. What is it?
